# Inference clusters: a reading note

Written 2026-10-25 as an orientation for Spliced. We measure joules per token and per correct answer on single laptop and desktop chips. This note is about what changes when the same workload runs across hundreds of accelerators, and what a two-person group with two machines could measure that would still count. Every number below is tagged as measured (a paper or a reproducible benchmark run), vendor claim (a datasheet, a marketing page, or a "projected" footnote), or derived (arithmetic we did from spec sheets). The short version: decode is a memory-bandwidth problem and prefill is a compute problem, so clusters now split them onto different machines and move the KV cache between them; the scale-up fabric (NVLink today, UALink and Ultra Ethernet as open alternatives) decides how wide tensor and expert parallelism can go; KV caches are pooled across DRAM and SSD and treated as a first-class resource; mixture-of-experts models are served with expert parallelism across dozens of GPUs; the only end-to-end energy numbers with a stated boundary come from Google, and they say the accelerator is 58% of a prompt's energy; speculative decoding helps at low load and shrinks at high load; and the honest experiments for us are energy rooflines, draft models under batching, KV reuse break-evens, and a two-box disaggregation microbenchmark.

## 1. Decode is bandwidth bound, prefill is compute bound

The roofline model ([Williams, Waterman, Patterson, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1145/1498765.1498785)) says a kernel is bandwidth bound when its arithmetic intensity (FLOPs per byte moved) is below the chip's ridge point (peak FLOPs divided by peak bandwidth). An H100 SXM has about 3.35 TB/s of HBM3 and about 990 TFLOPS of dense BF16, a ridge near 300 FLOP/byte (derived from the datasheet). Decoding one token for one sequence reads every weight once and does two FLOPs per weight, an intensity near 1 FLOP/byte. Prefill processes thousands of prompt tokens against the same weights, so intensity scales with prompt length and quickly passes the ridge. [Pope et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.05102) (measured, TPU v4) is the clearest treatment across chips and parallelism layouts.

The consequence is that decode throughput grows almost linearly with batch size until the batch is large enough to make the step compute bound, because the weight read is amortised across sequences. This is why serving systems fight for batch. [Orca](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi22/presentation/yu) (OSDI 2022, measured) introduced iteration-level scheduling, now called continuous batching: requests join and leave the batch every token step rather than waiting for the whole batch to finish. It reports 36.9x throughput over FasterTransformer on GPT-3 175B at equal latency, against request-granularity batching. [vLLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06180) (SOSP 2023, measured) added PagedAttention so KV memory stops being the thing that caps batch size, and reports 2 to 4x over the prior continuous-batching systems.

Two things follow at cluster scale. The KV cache, not the weights, becomes the memory consumer, since each sequence in the batch drags its own cache. And batch size is a latency trade: more tokens per second per GPU, fewer per user, which is why serious benchmarks report throughput at a fixed interactivity rather than peak throughput.

On our own chips the same roofline holds with smaller numbers. AMD lists Strix Halo at 256 GB/s LPDDR5X (vendor spec). The RTX 5070 Ti laptop GPU is 192-bit GDDR7, roughly 670 GB/s (derived from the spec). Batch-1 decode sits in the same regime as on an H100, just slower, which is why our single-chip measurements transfer.

## 2. Disaggregated prefill and decode

Running both phases on one GPU makes each worse: a long prefill stalls every decoding sequence in the batch, and interleaved decode steps lower prefill efficiency. The fix is separate machines, with the KV cache shipped from the prefill pool to the decode pool.

[DistServe](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi24/presentation/zhong-yinmin) (OSDI 2024, measured, A100) frames the goal as goodput: requests per second per GPU that meet both a time-to-first-token and a time-per-output-token target. It picks the parallelism for each phase separately and reports up to 7.4x more requests served, or a 12.6x tighter SLO, versus vLLM at the same latency targets. The "up to" matters; the gain depends on SLO and workload.

[Splitwise](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/splitwise-efficient-generative-llm-inference-using-phase-splitting/) (ISCA 2024, measured, H100 and A100) makes the economic point explicit: decode under-uses compute, so it can run on older or cheaper GPUs. It reports 1.4x throughput at 20% lower cost, or 2.35x throughput at the same cost and power budget, for Llama 2 70B and BLOOM 176B. It also measures power per phase, which is why it matters for us.

[Mooncake](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00079) (FAST 2025, measured in production at Kimi) separates the clusters and adds a disaggregated KV store across CPU DRAM and SSD. The 525% figure in its README is from simulated long-context scenarios; the production number is 75% more requests served under SLOs. Those are different claims and should be quoted separately.

[NVIDIA Dynamo](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/introducing-nvidia-dynamo-a-low-latency-distributed-inference-framework-for-scaling-reasoning-ai-models) is the productised version. Its "up to 30x" for DeepSeek-R1 on GB200 NVL72 is a vendor claim against a non-disaggregated TensorRT-LLM baseline and is footnoted as projected. The independent [SemiAnalysis InferenceX comparison](https://inferencex.semianalysis.com/blog/gb200-nvl72-vs-b200-disagg-deepseek-r1-fp4-dynamo-trt) of GB200 NVL72 against B200, both disaggregated, finds up to 4.39x per GPU at matched interactivity, which is a fairer number.

The cost is the KV transfer. For a Llama 3 70B style model with 8 KV heads, 80 layers and 128-dim heads, the cache is 2 x 80 x 8 x 128 x 2 bytes, about 320 KB per token in BF16, so 2.6 GB for an 8k prompt (derived). DistServe's argument is that this is small relative to prefill compute time when the link is NVLink or 400G InfiniBand, and the transfer can overlap layer by layer. That argument weakens as link speed falls, which is one of the things a two-box setup can actually measure (section 8).

## 3. Scale-up domains and interconnects

Tensor parallelism (TP) splits each layer across GPUs and needs an all-reduce per layer per token step; at decode the messages are small, so latency and per-message overhead matter, not peak bandwidth. Expert parallelism (EP) needs an all-to-all dispatch and combine per MoE layer; volume scales with batch and hidden size, so bandwidth matters more. The size of the fast domain therefore decides the layout.

NVIDIA GB200 NVL72 (vendor spec, [NVIDIA](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/gb200-nvl72/)) puts 72 Blackwell GPUs in one NVLink 5 domain at 1.8 TB/s per GPU (bidirectional) and 130 TB/s aggregate, with 13.5 TB of HBM3e. The point is that 72 GPUs share one fabric without crossing InfiniBand. [DeepEP's README](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepEP) (measured, H800) gives the practical ratio: about 160 GB/s per GPU over NVLink inside a node against about 50 GB/s over a 400 Gb/s NIC between nodes; its all-to-all kernels reach 153 GB/s intra-node and 43 to 46 GB/s inter-node. That 3x to 4x cliff at the node edge is what wide EP is designed around. SemiAnalysis notes NVL72 lets decode run EP=32 inside one NVLink domain where an 8-GPU box caps at EP=8.

AMD's [MI300X platform datasheet](https://www.amd.com/content/dam/amd/en/documents/instinct-tech-docs/data-sheets/amd-instinct-mi300x-platform-data-sheet.pdf) (vendor spec) is a mesh of 8 GPUs with seven 128 GB/s bidirectional Infinity Fabric links per GPU (896 GB/s per GPU), 5.3 TB/s HBM3 and 192 GB each. The [MI355X brochure](https://www.amd.com/content/dam/amd/en/documents/instinct-tech-docs/product-briefs/amd-instinct-miI355x-platform-brochure.pdf) moves to 288 GB HBM3E at 8 TB/s per GPU, with seven links at 144 GB/s each by AMD's figure (other summaries say 153.6 GB/s), so 1.0 to 1.1 TB/s per GPU. The 8-GPU domain is the main structural gap to NVL72, and AMD's answer is UALink.

[UALink 200G 1.0](https://www.hpcwire.com/aiwire/2025/04/08/ualink-consortium-releases-the-ultra-accelerator-link-200g-1-0-specification/) (spec, April 2025) defines 200G per lane, 800 Gb/s per port, up to 1,024 accelerators per pod, and claims sub-microsecond round trips under 4 m; no silicon has been measured independently. [Ultra Ethernet 1.0](https://ultraethernet.org/) (spec, June 2025) is the scale-out side: packet spraying, out-of-order delivery and new congestion control, with in-network collectives deferred.

Vendors quote sub-microsecond wire latency; what a TP all-reduce sees is end to end. [One NCCL study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.15076) (measured, H100) reports 16 to 17 microseconds round trip for small RDMA messages, and intra-node collectives floor in the tens of microseconds from launch overhead. With 80 layers and two all-reduces each, 20 microseconds apiece is 3.2 ms per token, 6% of the budget at 20 tokens/s per user. That is the number to keep in mind, not 1.8 TB/s.

## 4. KV cache management across a cluster

PagedAttention ([vLLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06180), measured) stores the KV cache in fixed-size blocks with an indirection table, so sequences do not need contiguous memory and blocks can be shared. Prefix caching builds on it: [SGLang's RadixAttention](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.07104) (measured) keeps the cache of every served prefix in a radix tree and reuses it when a new request shares a prefix (system prompts, multi-turn chat, agent loops). Both are standard in every engine now.

At cluster scale the cache leaves the GPU. Mooncake's store uses idle CPU DRAM and SSD across the cluster as a pooled KV tier, so a prefill node can fetch a prefix computed elsewhere instead of recomputing it. The production evidence that this is worth it is in DeepSeek's [inference system overview](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/open-infra-index/blob/main/202502OpenSourceWeek/day_6_one_more_thing_deepseekV3R1_inference_system_overview.md) (measured, 24 hours in February 2025): of 608 billion input tokens, 342 billion hit the on-disk KV cache, a 56.3% hit rate. That is more than half of all prefill compute avoided, which is why they price cached input at a quarter of uncached.

[LMCache](https://github.com/LMCache/LMCache) is the open-source layer for this (CPU RAM, local disk, Redis, GPUDirect Storage backends), and NVIDIA's [Dynamo KVBM](https://docs.nvidia.com/dynamo/dev/knowledge-base/modular-components/kvbm/overview) does the same with host, disk and object storage tiers over its NIXL transfer library. Offload performance claims are mostly vendor blogs (a VAST and CoreWeave post claims 20x TTFT improvement versus recomputing long histories) and are best case. The honest framing is a break-even: fetching a block from a slower tier costs bandwidth and latency; recomputing it costs prefill FLOPs. Which wins depends on prefix length, link speed and how busy the prefill pool is. DeepSeek's hit rate says the store pays for itself in chat; it does not say where the break-even sits on a given link, and nobody has published that in energy.

The quantity to track is bytes of KV per token: 320 KB for a 70B GQA model in BF16 (derived above), far less for DeepSeek's MLA, which compresses the KV to a 512-dim latent per token, and that compression is a large part of why DeepSeek can afford EP144 decode with an average KV length of 4,989 tokens per output token.

## 5. MoE serving at scale

A mixture-of-experts model activates a few experts per token (DeepSeek-V3: 8 of 256 per layer), so its FLOPs per token are a fraction of its parameter count, but every expert must still be resident somewhere. Tensor-parallel MoE replicates all experts on every GPU and wastes memory; expert parallelism (EP) gives each GPU a few experts and routes tokens to them with an all-to-all. Wide EP spreads experts over dozens or hundreds of GPUs so each holds few experts and batches many tokens through them, raising arithmetic intensity on the expert matmuls.

DeepSeek's write-up (measured, H800, [link above](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/open-infra-index/blob/main/202502OpenSourceWeek/day_6_one_more_thing_deepseekV3R1_inference_system_overview.md)) is the clearest public account. Prefill units are 4 nodes (32 GPUs) at EP32 with 9 routed experts plus 1 shared per GPU. Decode units are 18 nodes (144 GPUs) at EP144 with 2 routed experts plus 1 shared per GPU. Redundant copies of hot experts and three load balancers (prefill, decode, expert) keep GPUs even. Communication is hidden by splitting each batch into two microbatches that alternate compute and all-to-all. The result over 24 hours was about 73.7k input tokens/s per node in prefill (including cache hits) and 14.8k output tokens/s per node in decode, at 20 to 22 tokens/s per user, across an average of 226.75 nodes. They quote $87k per day of cost at $2 per GPU-hour against $562k of theoretical revenue, a hypothetical since much traffic is free or discounted.

The open reproduction is [SGLang on 96 H100s](https://lmsys.org/blog/2025-05-05-large-scale-ep/) (measured): prefill-decode disaggregation plus large-scale EP with DeepEP and DeepGEMM reached 52.3k input and 22.3k output tokens/s per node for 2,000-token inputs, up to 5x over plain tensor parallelism on the same hardware. The [GB200 NVL72 follow-up](https://lmsys.org/blog/2025-09-25-gb200-part-2) (measured) gets 26,156 prefill and 13,386 decode tokens/s per GPU with FP8 attention and NVFP4 experts at 48-rank decode EP, which they quote as 3.8x and 4.8x over the H100 per GPU. Note that the authors credit most of the gain to precision and kernels, not to the bigger NVLink domain, which they do not isolate. NVIDIA's own [wide-EP post](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/?p=107418) is a vendor description of the same technique in TensorRT-LLM.

The lesson for cluster design is that wide EP trades memory per GPU for all-to-all traffic, and it only works when that traffic stays inside the fast domain or is hidden behind compute. The DeepEP bandwidths in section 3 are the budget.

## 6. Energy at cluster scale

The only end-to-end figure with a stated boundary is Google's ([Google, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.15734), measured in production): a median Gemini Apps text prompt in May 2025 used 0.24 Wh, split 58% active accelerators (0.14 Wh), 25% host CPU and DRAM (0.06 Wh), 10% idle machines (0.02 Wh) and 8% data-center overhead at a fleet PUE of 1.09. The accelerator-only number, the way most benchmarks measure, would be 0.10 Wh, so a benchmark-style figure undercounts by 2.4x. They also report a 33x fall per median prompt over 12 months, mostly software and model changes; a per-token number without a date is not a number. No tokens per prompt are given, so it cannot be converted to joules per token.

MLPerf Inference has an optional power category, and it is sparse: in v4.0 only Dell, Fujitsu, NVIDIA and Qualcomm submitted data-center power ([IEEE Spectrum](https://spectrum.ieee.org/ai-benchmark-mlperf-llama-stablediffusion)). The [MLCommons tables](https://mlcommons.org/benchmarks/inference-datacenter/) give system watts next to tokens/s, so tokens per joule is the reader's arithmetic. SemiAnalysis's InferenceX lists tokens per second per megawatt (around 2.9 million for GB200 NVL72 against 1.4 million for MI355X at one operating point), derived from rated power, not measured draw.

Vendor claims are the loudest numbers and the least usable. NVIDIA's "25x lower energy" for GB200 NVL72 is a projected comparison against air-cooled H100 over InfiniBand on a 1.8T MoE at a fixed latency target ([datasheet footnote](https://openzeka.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/blackwell-datasheet.pdf)); nobody has reproduced it.

What dominates, from measured work: the ML.ENERGY benchmark ([Chung et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.06371), measured, H100) shows energy per response falling 3.5x to 5.8x as max batch size rises from 4 to 64 for reasoning models, and that TDP-based estimates overstate energy by up to 4.1x. [POLCA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.12908) (ASPLOS 2024, measured at Microsoft) found LLM inference clusters leave about 21% power headroom at cluster level even with high server peaks, and [DynamoLLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.00741) (HPCA 2025, measured) saved 53% energy by resizing instances, parallelism and GPU clocks against demand. [Samsi et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.03003) (measured, A100 and V100) show the same batch effect on LLaMA and explore power caps. The pattern: underutilisation is the biggest lever an operator controls, then batch, then clocks. Cooling is a fixed 9% at Google's PUE. The split of accelerator energy between HBM traffic and compute is not published as a measurement for any current chip; it is inferred from the roofline.

## 7. Speculative decoding at cluster scale

Speculative decoding ([Leviathan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.17192), [Chen et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.01318)) uses a cheap draft to propose k tokens and the target model to verify them in one forward pass. It works because batch-1 decode is bandwidth bound: verifying k tokens costs about the same weight read as generating one, so the speedup depends on how far below the ridge the target step sits.

That is also why batching erodes it. [Su et al., "The Synergy of Speculative Decoding and Batching"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.18813) (measured, RTX 3090, OPT-6.7B with a 125M draft) found the optimal speculation length shrinks with batch size: 2.73x at batch 1, 1.31x at batch 32, with longer drafts causing slowdowns at batch 16 and 32. The [vLLM team's measurements](https://blog.vllm.ai/2024/10/17/spec-decode.html) (measured, Llama 3 70B on 4 H100s) show up to 1.5x with a draft model and 2.8x with n-gram lookup at 1 query per second, and say plainly that at high QPS speculation can cost throughput because the system is already compute bound. [SmartSpec](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.14066) makes speculation length a per-request decision, down to zero, by predicting goodput.

The counter-case is long context. [MagicDec](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.11049) (measured, 8 A100s) shows that at long sequences the KV cache read, not the weights, dominates decode, so it stays bandwidth bound even at batch 32 to 256, and a draft with a sparse KV cache gives up to 2.51x on Llama 3.1 8B. DeepSeek-V3 ships a multi-token-prediction head and reports 85 to 90% acceptance of the second token and about 1.8x tokens per second ([technical report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.19437), measured); [EAGLE-3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01840) is the current feature-level draft method.

For a draft-model builder: a draft is worth most where the deployment is latency bound (low load, long context, single-user devices) and least in a saturated cluster. Energy should follow the same curve but has not been measured; every paper above reports speed.

## 8. What we could measure first

Each of these is chosen so the result holds on its own, without pretending two machines are a cluster.

1. Energy roofline for decode. Joules per token at the wall against batch size (1 to 64) and context length for one model on the M5 Pro, Strix Halo and the 5070 Ti; fit the knee where J/token stops falling. Honest because it is a per-chip curve, ML.ENERGY shows TDP estimates are off by up to 4x, and three memory systems (LPDDR5X unified, GDDR7 discrete, Apple unified) is a real data point. Needs: a wall-power meter and an engine that exposes batch size.

2. Draft models under batching, in joules. Repeat Su et al. on our hardware but record energy per accepted token for our own drafts at batch 1, 4, 16, 32 and at short versus long context. Honest because nothing published reports the energy side of the batching cliff, and drafts are what we build. Needs: the 5070 Ti (CUDA, best engine support) and the Halo (unified memory, big batches fit).

3. KV reuse break-even. Energy and TTFT of a prefix-cache hit from RAM and from NVMe against recomputing, for prefixes from 256 to 32k tokens. The output is the prefix length where fetching beats recomputing, in joules and milliseconds, per tier. Honest because a Mooncake-style scheduler needs this number and nobody has published it with energy; the tiers differ from a cluster's but the method transfers. Needs: either box plus LMCache or SGLang's hierarchical cache.

4. Two-box disaggregation microbenchmark. Prefill on one machine, decode on the other, over 10 GbE or Thunderbolt. Measure KV transfer time against prefill time as prompt length grows, and compute the link bandwidth at which transfer stops being hidden. Honest as a scaling-law point: it claims no cluster numbers, it checks DistServe's "transfer is negligible" assumption and reports where it breaks. Needs: both boxes, a wired link, vLLM or Dynamo with the disaggregated connector.

5. Calibrated simulation. Take an open serving simulator ([Vidur](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05465), MLSys 2024, or Splitwise's), replace its per-operator timing tables with our measured timing and energy, then run cluster policies (batching, disaggregation ratios, EP widths) and report sensitivities, not absolutes. Honest only while every output is labelled as simulated from laptop-calibrated inputs.

Experiments 1 and 2 are the ones to start, because they extend what we already do and produce curves other people can compare against.

## What we do not know

- Joules per token for any production cluster. Google gives Wh per prompt without tokens; DeepSeek gives tokens per node without power; MLPerf power results exist but no submitter publishes a per-token figure.
- The split of accelerator energy between HBM traffic, interconnect and compute on Blackwell or MI355X. It is inferred, never measured in public.
- Whether the GB200 NVL72 gains over H100 come from the 72-GPU NVLink domain or from FP4 and kernels. SGLang's authors do not isolate it.
- Where the KV fetch-versus-recompute break-even sits on any link, in energy.
- UALink latency in silicon. The spec says under 1 microsecond; nothing has shipped to measure.
- Whether speculative decoding reduces energy, not just latency, at any batch size.
- How much of NVIDIA's "25x" and "30x" survives a fair baseline. The independent comparison is 4.4x, and against a different baseline.
